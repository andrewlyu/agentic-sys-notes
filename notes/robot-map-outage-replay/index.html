When Sensor Updates Stop: Replaying a Robot-Map Outage

My robot vacuum inspired a useful question: what happens when a robot keeps running, but its picture of the room stops updating? I added a scripted sensor-delivery outage to my robot-map simulator to make that question observable—and repeatable.

Dashboard highlight: At 11 simulated seconds, observation delivery is unavailable and the robot is holding. The last discovered map stays visible, while its age, pending observations and retry attempts show that updates are no longer arriving normally.

From a working map to a failure experiment

In my previous experiment, I used a public robot-map sample as a hidden floor plan. A software robot explores it through a virtual range sensor, building the discovered map shown on the dashboard. The sample is an existing vendor-specific map, not a recording from my home.

This extension adds a failure between observation generation and delivery. The sensor keeps producing observations, but delivery temporarily fails. Pending observations retry, and the robot must decide whether the data it has accepted is still recent enough to move.

The deliverable is a repeatable failure-testing tool: I can inject a known incident, inspect the consuming system’s response and replay the same case after a change.

One deliberate outage

The failure is predetermined. In a 20-second replay, observation delivery is unavailable from 10 seconds up to, but not including, 15 seconds. These are simulated seconds; pausing the replay pauses its clock.

  • 9 seconds: delivery is normal, observations are current and the robot is moving.
  • 10 seconds: the scripted delivery outage begins; retries start accumulating.
  • 10.9 seconds: the newest accepted observation reaches one second old, so the robot holds.
  • 15 seconds: fresh observations are accepted again, releasing the hold.
  • 20 seconds: the replay is complete and the pending queue is empty.

Before the outage: At 9 simulated seconds, the dashboard shows current observations, normal delivery and no pending scans.

Holding is the policy I chose for this experiment. It makes the stale-data boundary visible; it is not a claim that a one-second threshold is suitable for a physical robot.

A retry does not make old data fresh

A scan captured at 10 seconds and delivered at 15 seconds is still five seconds old. Treating its arrival as a new capture would hide the outage from the consumer.

I preserve each observation’s capture time and sequence through retries. Retries run every half-second within bounded limits, and an older sequence cannot replace a newer accepted observation. The discovered map remains on screen during the hold, but its presence does not imply that it is current.

That distinction is the useful part of the dashboard. Delivery state, observation age and robot movement are separate signals. The retry counter and pending queue explain what the pipeline is doing; the event timeline connects those attempts to holding and recovery.

After recovery: At 20 simulated seconds, delivery is normal, no scans remain pending and the completed replay passes verification. Fresh accepted observations released the hold at 15 seconds.

Evidence beyond a reassuring animation

The replay exports its configuration, map provenance and event trace. Its verifier checks the order of delivery and acceptance, observation freshness, stationary holding and actual movement after recovery. A “moving” label alone is insufficient evidence, and incomplete traces cannot establish a pass.

The local dashboard workflow passed 62 automated tests across the model, API and browser. Three fresh executions of this fixed replay also produced identical exported traces, each passing verification. That gives me a reproducible baseline for this incident; it does not establish reliability across arbitrary failures.

Next: varied faults that I can replay

The next extension I’m considering is seeded random fault generation: vary when an outage starts and how long it lasts, then save both the seed and the resulting schedule. An interesting failure could become a named regression case instead of a story about something that happened once.

That extension is not implemented yet. Its purpose would be broader exploration with reproducible logical fault schedules; real network timing would require separate treatment.

For now, this is a local, in-process simulation with idealized sensing. It does not test a physical vacuum or a live network. It is a concrete step toward the simulation testbed I want to build: a place to inject data-delivery incidents, observe their consequences and keep evidence of how the consuming system responds. The vacuum still has the easier job—it only has to clean the floor.
